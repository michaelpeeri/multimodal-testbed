## Can arbitrary data be suitable training data for a DEQ?

**Yes, with important caveats.** The key insight is that the DEQ architecture imposes constraints on what the *model* computes, not on what the *data* must look like. Here's the reasoning:

### What a DEQ actually learns

A DEQ finds a fixed point `z* = f(z*, x)` where `x` is the input. The model is trained to produce outputs that minimize some loss on the training data. The fixed-point structure is a *computational mechanism*, not a constraint on the data-generating process.

Analogy: a recurrent network can be trained on static image classification even though images weren't generated by a recurrent process. The architecture's inductive bias may help or hurt, but it doesn't make the data "invalid."

### When a dataset is *well-suited* for a DEQ

A dataset tends to be a good fit when the underlying mapping `x -> y` has properties that fixed-point iteration exploits well:

1. **The target relationship has implicit/self-referential structure** - e.g., outputs that satisfy some equilibrium condition (physics simulations, steady-state systems, constraint satisfaction problems).
2. **The data lives on a manifold that fixed-point solvers navigate efficiently** - smooth, well-conditioned mappings where iterative refinement converges quickly.
3. **The Jacobian of the learned `f` at the fixed point is well-conditioned** - if spectral radius of `∂f/∂z` at `z*` is < 1, the iteration contracts, which is both trainable and interpretable.

### Formal sufficient condition

For a dataset `{(x_i, y_i)}`, a DEQ is *learnable* on it if there exists a function `f` parameterized by your network such that:
- `f(y_i, x_i) ≈ y_i` (the targets are approximate fixed points of `f` conditioned on inputs)
- The fixed-point iteration converges in the forward pass (Banach fixed-point theorem: `f` must be a contraction near `z*`)

The first condition is what training actually enforces. The second is what makes the forward solver tractable.

### Practical implication for your synthetic data

If your synthetic data is generated by *any* deterministic or stochastic process `g: x -> y`, you can ask:

- **Is there a contraction `f` such that `y = f(y, x)`?** Almost always yes - you can always define `f(z, x) = (1-α)z + α·h(x)` for some `h` and small enough `α`, making it a contraction by construction. Whether your *network* can learn this `f` efficiently is a separate question.
- **Does the DEQ's inductive bias match the data structure?** If `y` has no equilibrium interpretation, the DEQ will still train, but it may be less parameter-efficient than a feedforward model since you're spending capacity on an unnecessary fixed-point mechanism.

### Bottom line

No dataset property is strictly *necessary* for DEQ training. What matters is:

1. **Convergence**: the learned `f` must be a contraction (this is enforced via Jacobian regularization or solver tolerance during training)
2. **Expressiveness**: `f` must be expressive enough to represent the mapping
3. **Efficiency**: DEQs are most *justified* when the data has implicit structure, but they're not *invalid* otherwise

If you're comparing DEQ against other architectures on the same synthetic data, the question becomes empirical: does the DEQ's fixed-point inductive bias help or hurt on *this particular* mapping? That's a property of the relationship between your data-generating process and the fixed-point formulation, and is best answered by your benchmark results.

## Constraints for Trainability of `z* = f(z*, x)`

There are three interlocking constraint families: on `z`, on `x`, and on the joint structure of `f`.

---

### 1. Constraints on `z` (the equilibrium state)

**Space constraints:**
- `z` must live in a complete metric space (e.g., a closed subset of `R^n`). This is required by the Banach fixed-point theorem — without completeness, a Cauchy sequence of iterates may not converge to a point *in* the space.
- In practice: `z ∈ R^n` is always complete, so this is free. But if you constrain `z` to lie on a manifold (e.g., a simplex, a sphere), you need the fixed-point map to respect that manifold.

**Initialization constraints:**
- The forward solver starts from some `z_0` (often `z_0 = 0`). For convergence, the contraction must hold in a neighborhood containing the path from `z_0` to `z*`. If the contraction is only *local*, the basin of attraction matters — a bad `z_0` can cause the solver to diverge or find the wrong fixed point.
- If `f` has *multiple fixed points* for a given `x`, the one found depends on `z_0`. This is a trainability hazard: the same `x` may yield different `z*` across training steps if initialization is near a basin boundary.

**Uniqueness constraint:**
- For clean supervised learning, you want a *unique* `z*` for each `x`. Non-uniqueness means the loss surface has discontinuities (different solver runs on the same `x` land on different `z*`), making gradients unreliable.

---

### 2. Constraints on `x` (the input/condition)

`x` enters `f` as a conditioning signal, not as a variable being iterated. The constraints on `x` are therefore about *how it modulates the fixed-point map*:

**Lipschitz conditioning:**
- The map `x -> z*(x)` (the *implicit function* induced by the fixed point) must be sufficiently smooth for the training loss to be differentiable through it. By the implicit function theorem, the derivative is:

  ```
  dz*/dx = -(I - ∂f/∂z)^{-1} · ∂f/∂x
  ```

  This requires `(I - ∂f/∂z)` to be invertible at `z*`, i.e., 1 must not be an eigenvalue of the Jacobian `∂f/∂z`. If the Jacobian has an eigenvalue at 1, the fixed point is *non-hyperbolic* and the implicit function theorem fails — gradients blow up.

**Distribution stability:**
- If `x` shifts distribution at test time, the fixed-point `z*(x)` may shift into a region where `f` is no longer a contraction. This is a generalization concern specific to DEQs: the contraction condition is not globally guaranteed, only in the region explored during training.

---

### 3. Constraints on `f` itself (the critical ones)

These are the constraints that training must enforce:

**Contraction (Banach condition):**
The core requirement. `f(·, x)` must be a contraction mapping in `z`:

```
‖f(z₁, x) - f(z₂, x)‖ ≤ L‖z₁ - z₂‖,  L < 1  for all z₁, z₂, x
```

Equivalently, the spectral radius of `∂f/∂z` must be < 1 at the fixed point (and ideally everywhere in the basin).

This is **not automatic** — a randomly initialized neural network will generally *not* be a contraction. Training must drive `L < 1` either via:
- **Jacobian regularization**: explicit penalty on `‖∂f/∂z‖`
- **Weight normalization / spectral normalization**: architectural enforcement
- **Monotone DEQ**: constraining `f` to be a monotone operator (stronger than contraction, but guarantees unique fixed point)

**The gradient problem (why this is subtle):**
Training uses backpropagation *through* the fixed point, not through the iterations. The implicit differentiation formula above requires solving a linear system `(I - J^T)v = ∂L/∂z*` (where `J = ∂f/∂z`) at every backward pass. If `J` has spectral radius near 1, this system is ill-conditioned — gradients are numerically unstable even if the forward pass converges.

So there are actually *two* spectral radius conditions:
- Forward: `ρ(∂f/∂z) < 1` for the solver to converge
- Backward: `ρ(∂f/∂z)` sufficiently less than 1 for implicit differentiation to be well-conditioned

---

### 4. The joint constraint: implicit function theorem requirements

Combining the above, the full trainability condition at a fixed point `z*` given `x`:

| Condition | What fails if violated |
|---|---|
| `f(z*, x) = z*` | No fixed point exists |
| `ρ(∂f/∂z\|_{z*}) < 1` | Forward solver diverges |
| `1 ∉ spectrum(∂f/∂z\|_{z*})` | Implicit gradient undefined |
| `(I - ∂f/∂z)` well-conditioned | Backward pass numerically unstable |
| Unique `z*` for each `x` | Loss surface discontinuous |
| `z*` in basin of `z_0` | Solver finds wrong fixed point |

---

### 5. What this means for your synthetic data

The data doesn't need to encode these properties — the *model* must learn them. But your data implicitly determines whether a well-conditioned fixed-point representation *exists*:

- If the mapping `x -> y` is highly non-smooth or multimodal, a contraction `f` with a unique fixed point may require very large `L` (close to 1), making training unstable.
- If the mapping is smooth and has low intrinsic dimensionality, there likely exists a well-conditioned `f` with `ρ(J) << 1`, and the DEQ will train cleanly.
- The *condition number* of the mapping — how sensitively `y` depends on `x` — is a proxy for how well-conditioned the DEQ's backward pass will be.

In short: the constraints are on the *learned `f`*, but the data determines whether a well-conditioned `f` *exists* that fits it.

## DEQ with Multiple Fixed Points: Data Trainability

Relaxing uniqueness fundamentally changes the problem. You are no longer learning a function `x -> y` but a *relation* `x -> {y_1, y_2, ..., y_k}`, and the DEQ must learn to represent the full fixed-point structure, not just one branch.

---

### What changes when multiple fixed points exist

The implicit function theorem derivation from before still applies *locally* — near each fixed point `z*_i`, if `ρ(∂f/∂z|_{z*_i}) < 1`, the fixed point is isolated and stable. The new structure is:

- `f(·, x)` has multiple stable fixed points `{z*_1, z*_2, ..., z*_k(x)}`, each with its own basin of attraction.
- The forward solver converges to whichever `z*_i` contains `z_0` in its basin.
- Between stable fixed points there must be at least one *unstable* fixed point (a basin boundary), where `ρ(∂f/∂z) ≥ 1`. These are repellers for the forward solver.

This is the topology of a *multi-stable dynamical system*, and it is legitimate to model this with a DEQ — `z_0` acts as a selector of which branch is found.

---

### Core trainability requirements (revised)

#### Per fixed point (local conditions)
Each stable fixed point `z*_i(x)` must individually satisfy the original conditions:
- `ρ(∂f/∂z|_{z*_i}) < 1` — stable, solver converges
- `1 ∉ spectrum(∂f/∂z|_{z*_i})` — implicit gradient defined
- `(I - ∂f/∂z|_{z*_i})` well-conditioned — backward pass stable

#### Global conditions (new, specific to multi-fixed-point case)

1. **Basin separation**: The basins of attraction of distinct fixed points must be robustly separated — not just topologically, but metrically. If two basins are nearly touching, small perturbations in `z_0` or numerical noise in the solver flip which branch is found, creating discontinuous loss gradients.

2. **Branch-consistent labeling in data**: Each data point `(x, y)` must be associated with a *specific branch*. If your dataset has `(x, y_1)` and `(x, y_2)` as separate examples without identifying which branch each belongs to, the model sees contradictory supervision for the same `x` — the loss will average across branches and the model will learn *neither* fixed point well, instead collapsing to something in between.

3. **Branch coverage**: If `f` has `k` stable fixed points for a given `x`, your training data should include examples from *all* branches, otherwise the model has no gradient signal to place those fixed points correctly.

4. **Initialization as a latent variable**: `z_0` must be treated as encoding the branch identity. This means either:
   - `z_0` is supplied as an input (making branch selection explicit), or
   - `z_0` is sampled from a distribution that covers all basins (stochastic initialization), or
   - the dataset includes `z_0` as a conditioning signal alongside `x`

   Without this, the solver always starts from the same `z_0` (e.g., zero) and can only ever find whichever branch contains that point — the other branches are unreachable and untrainable.

---

### What "trainable data" means in this setting

A dataset `{(x_i, y_i)}` is trainable for a multi-fixed-point DEQ if and only if:

1. **Branch labels are available or recoverable**: Each `(x_i, y_i)` is tagged with which branch `b_i ∈ {1,...,k}` it belongs to. This is equivalent to saying the data was generated by a process that tracks which attractor was reached — not necessarily iteratively, but with branch identity known.

2. **Each branch is identified by a `z_0` that lands in its basin**: The dataset (or your initialization scheme) must supply a `z_0` per example such that the solver started at `z_0` converges to the `y_i` of that example. Concretely, `z_0` can simply be `y_i` itself (or a noisy version), which is guaranteed to be in the correct basin. This is analogous to *warm starting* the solver on each training example.

3. **The fixed points are isolated (not connected by a path of stable points)**: If your data-generating process produces a continuous family of equilibria (a line or manifold of fixed points), the Jacobian at each point necessarily has an eigenvalue at 1 (the tangent direction), violating the implicit gradient condition everywhere. Isolated fixed points are required.

4. **No branch merges or bifurcations within the training distribution**: If as `x` varies, two fixed points merge and annihilate (a saddle-node bifurcation), then near the bifurcation point the basin boundary approaches the stable fixed point, the Jacobian eigenvalue approaches 1, and gradients blow up. Your `x` distribution should avoid bifurcation points, or at minimum you should not expect stable gradients near them.

---

### Practical data generation recipe

Given all of the above, if you are generating synthetic data for this setting, the following ensures trainability:

1. **Generate `(x, z*_i)` pairs by running the true dynamics to convergence** from `k` distinct initializations per `x`, and label each by which basin it converged to. This directly gives you branch-labeled data.

2. **Verify basin separation**: Check that `‖z*_i - z*_j‖` for `i ≠ j` is bounded away from zero across your `x` distribution, and that the Jacobian spectral radii at each fixed point stay well below 1.

3. **Avoid the bifurcation locus**: Map out (or bound) where in `x`-space fixed points merge, and exclude or down-weight those regions.

4. **Store `z*_i` (or a noisy version) as the initialization `z_0`** to pair with each training example, so the solver is always warm-started in the correct basin.

5. **Balance branch coverage**: Ensure each branch has adequate representation in the dataset, otherwise the model under-fits low-frequency branches.

---

### The fundamental insight

In the multi-fixed-point setting, the "suitability" of training data is not just a property of `(x, y)` pairs — it requires a *third variable*: the branch identity, operationalized as the solver initialization `z_0`. A dataset without this information is genuinely ambiguous for a DEQ in a way it would not be for a standard network, because the same `x` legitimately maps to multiple `y` values and the architecture resolves the ambiguity through `z_0`. If your data generation does not preserve and expose this information, the training signal is contradictory at the fixed-point level, and no amount of architecture tuning will fix it.

## Unsupervised DEQ Training with Multiple Fixed Points

Yes, this is possible, but it requires reframing the problem significantly. Without branch labels, you cannot use standard supervised loss directly — instead you must use the *structure of the fixed-point landscape itself* as a learning signal.

---

### The core difficulty

Without branch labels, your dataset is a set of observations `{y_i}` (possibly conditioned on `{x_i}`) where each `y_i` is *some* fixed point of the true system, but you don't know which. The model must simultaneously:

1. Learn `f` such that all observed `y_i` are fixed points: `f(y_i, x_i) = y_i`
2. Learn the basin structure without being told which basin each `y_i` belongs to
3. Not collapse all fixed points into one (mode collapse)

---

### Approach 1: Fixed-Point Loss Without Branch Labels

The most direct approach: supervise only the fixed-point condition, not the branch.

**Loss:**
```
L = ‖f(y_i, x_i) - y_i‖²
```

This says "every observed `y_i` should be a fixed point of `f`" without specifying which one. The model is free to learn a multi-stable `f` that has all observed `y_i` as attractors.

**What this gives you:** `f` will try to make every training point a fixed point. If your data genuinely samples from multiple branches, `f` will be pressured to create multiple attractors.

**The collapse problem:** Without additional regularization, the easiest solution is a flat `f(z, x) = c` (a constant), which makes *every* point a fixed point trivially. Or `f(z, x) = z` (identity), same issue. You need regularization to prevent this.

**Anti-collapse regularizers:**
- Penalize `‖∂f/∂z - I‖` being too small — force `f` to be nontrivial
- Penalize low variance of fixed points found from random `z_0` — force the model to produce diverse attractors
- Energy-based repulsion between fixed points found in a batch

---

### Approach 2: Energy-Based / Implicit Score Matching

Reframe the problem: instead of learning `f` directly, learn an *energy function* `E(z, x)` whose local minima are the fixed points. This naturally handles multiple modes.

- Fixed points become energy minima: `∇_z E(z*, x) = 0`
- `f` is then defined as a gradient descent step: `f(z, x) = z - α ∇_z E(z, x)`, which is automatically a contraction near minima when `E` is strongly convex there
- Training uses score matching or contrastive divergence to shape `E` so that observed `y_i` are low-energy

**Advantage:** Energy-based models are well-studied for unsupervised multi-modal learning (EBMs, diffusion models are a special case). The branch structure emerges from the energy landscape without explicit labels.

**Disadvantage:** Training EBMs is notoriously unstable; you need MCMC or other sampling to estimate the partition function gradient.

---

### Approach 3: Contrastive Fixed-Point Learning

Borrow from contrastive learning. For each observed `y_i`:

- **Positive**: `y_i` should satisfy `f(y_i, x_i) ≈ y_i` (it is a fixed point)
- **Negative**: random `z ~ p(z)` should *not* satisfy this: `‖f(z, x_i) - z‖` should be large

This shapes `f` to have fixed points concentrated near the data, and not elsewhere — a contrastive version of the fixed-point loss.

**Key insight:** This is equivalent to learning an implicit energy where the fixed-point residual `‖f(z,x) - z‖` acts as the energy. Data points are low-energy; random points are high-energy.

---

### Approach 4: Variational / Latent-Variable Formulation

Treat branch identity as a *latent variable* and marginalize over it — this is the most principled unsupervised approach.

Introduce a latent `b` (branch index or a continuous embedding of it) and model:
```
p(y | x) = Σ_b p(y | x, b) p(b | x)
```

where `p(y | x, b)` is the DEQ's output distribution when initialized at `z_0(b)`.

Train with **EM** or a **VAE-style ELBO**:
- **E-step**: infer `p(b | y, x)` — which branch does this observation most likely belong to?
- **M-step**: update `f` to make `z*(z_0(b), x)` fit `y` for the inferred branch

This is structurally identical to a *mixture model* where the DEQ plays the role of each mixture component, and `b` is the mixture index. The E-step is done by running the solver forward from multiple `z_0` and seeing which converges closest to `y_i`.

**Practical version:**
- Sample `K` initializations `{z_0^1, ..., z_0^K}` per training example
- Run the solver from each, get `{z*^1, ..., z*^K}`
- Assign `y_i` to the nearest `z*^j` (hard EM) or compute a soft assignment
- Backpropagate loss only through the assigned branch (or softly weighted)

This is the *closest analog to k-means for DEQs*.

---

### Approach 5: Lyapunov / Monotone Constraints as Structure

If you have prior knowledge about the *number* of fixed points or their rough locations (without branch labels per data point), you can encode this as architectural constraints:

- Use a **monotone DEQ** with a structured perturbation that creates exactly `k` fixed points by construction (e.g., a sum of `k` contraction mappings with separated attractors)
- The data then supervises the *locations* of these attractors without needing to label which attractor each point belongs to

---

### What makes unsupervised training tractable: key conditions on data

Even without branch labels, the data must satisfy structural conditions for any of the above to work:

| Condition | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Fixed points are well-separated in `z`-space | Allows the model to distinguish branches without labels; if branches overlap, no unsupervised method can separate them |
| Each branch is represented in data | A branch with zero data coverage has no gradient signal regardless of method |
| The data distribution is not unimodal around a branch boundary | If data clusters near an unstable fixed point, the model receives contradictory signal |
| `x` is informative about which branches exist | If `x` is uninformative, `f` cannot modulate the fixed-point structure meaningfully |

---

### Bottom line

The most practical unsupervised approach is the **latent-variable / soft EM formulation** (Approach 4): run the solver from multiple initializations per training example, assign each observation to the nearest fixed point found, and backpropagate through that branch. This recovers branch structure from the data geometry alone, without explicit labels, as long as the fixed points are sufficiently separated that nearest-neighbor assignment is consistent. The fundamental limitation is identical to that of unsupervised clustering: if branches are not geometrically separated in `z`-space, no method — supervised or not — can reliably distinguish them.

## DEQ as a Latent Variable Model for Noisy/Incomplete Observations

This reframing is a significant and important shift. You are no longer asking "which fixed point does this observation belong to?" but rather "given a noisy or incomplete observation, what is the *posterior distribution* over latent states `z` that are consistent with it?" This is a fundamentally generative / Bayesian inference problem.

---

### The reframed model

Instead of a deterministic fixed-point selector, you want:

```
p(z | x_obs) ∝ p(x_obs | z) · p(z)
```

where:
- `z` is the latent state (the fixed point, or something close to it)
- `x_obs` is an incomplete or noisy observation
- `p(z)` is a prior over latent states — ideally the *learned distribution of fixed points*
- `p(x_obs | z)` is a likelihood — how well does latent `z` explain the observation

The DEQ's role shifts: instead of being a *solver* that finds one fixed point, it becomes a component that *defines the prior `p(z)`* — the distribution of plausible latent states is the distribution of fixed points of `f`.

---

### Why this is a natural fit for a DEQ

The distribution of fixed points of a learned `f(z, x)` is not a simple parametric distribution — it is implicitly defined by the dynamics of the system. This is exactly the kind of structured, complex prior that generative models struggle to represent with explicit parametric families. A DEQ can represent:

- A prior concentrated on a low-dimensional manifold (the fixed-point manifold)
- A multi-modal prior (multiple fixed-point attractors)
- A prior whose geometry depends on `x` (the conditioning input shifts the fixed-point landscape)

This is closely related to **implicit generative models** and **energy-based models**, but with the fixed-point structure providing an additional inductive bias.

---

### The connection to VAEs and amortized inference

Your description maps almost exactly onto a **VAE with a structured prior**:

| VAE concept | DEQ analog |
|---|---|
| Latent `z` | Fixed-point state `z*` |
| Prior `p(z)` | Distribution of fixed points of `f` |
| Encoder `q(z\|x_obs)` | Inference network: maps noisy `x_obs` to a distribution over `z*` |
| Decoder `p(x_obs\|z)` | Observation model: maps `z*` to predicted observations |
| ELBO training | Balances reconstruction and KL to fixed-point prior |

The key difference from a standard VAE is that the prior is *not* a simple Gaussian — it is the fixed-point distribution of `f`, which has the multi-modal structure you want to learn.

---

### How the fixed-point prior encodes the multi-modal structure

In a standard VAE, the prior `p(z) = N(0, I)` is unimodal and doesn't capture the fact that realistic latents cluster around multiple modes. Your fixed-point distribution naturally captures this:

- Each stable fixed point `z*_i(x)` is an attractor — the prior is concentrated near these points
- The basins of attraction define the *effective support* of each mode
- The relative basin sizes (or frequencies in your data) encode the prior probability of each mode
- As `x` varies, the fixed-point landscape shifts, giving a conditional prior `p(z | x)` that varies meaningfully

This is structurally similar to a **diffusion model**, where the prior is defined by a learned denoising process rather than an analytic distribution — your DEQ iteration plays the role of the denoising chain.

---

### Inference: assigning a realistic latent to a noisy observation

Given a partial or noisy observation `x_obs`, inference means finding `z*` such that:

1. `z*` is a (approximate) fixed point of `f` — it is "realistic" under the learned prior
2. `z*` is consistent with `x_obs` under the likelihood `p(x_obs | z*)`

This is **posterior inference in an implicit prior model**, and there are several tractable approaches:

**Langevin / MCMC sampling:**
Run Langevin dynamics in `z`-space combining the fixed-point attraction (from `f`) and the likelihood gradient (from `x_obs`):
```
z_{t+1} = z_t - α ∇_z [‖f(z_t, ·) - z_t‖² - log p(x_obs | z_t)] + noise
```
The fixed-point residual acts as the energy of the prior; the likelihood term pulls toward observations. At convergence, samples are approximately from `p(z | x_obs)`.

**Amortized inference (encoder network):**
Train a separate encoder `q_φ(z | x_obs)` that directly predicts a distribution over `z*` given `x_obs`. Train jointly with `f` using an ELBO:
```
L = E_q[log p(x_obs | z)] - KL[q_φ(z | x_obs) ‖ p_f(z)]
```
The KL term requires evaluating the fixed-point prior `p_f(z)`, which is implicit — this is the hard part, requiring e.g. score matching or contrastive estimation.

**Fixed-point refinement as inference:**
Initialize `z_0` from the encoder's mean prediction, then run the DEQ solver forward. The solver refines the initial estimate toward the nearest fixed point, acting as a "snapping" operation that projects the encoder's output onto the fixed-point manifold. This is a natural two-stage inference: encoder gives a rough latent, DEQ refines it to be consistent with the learned dynamics.

---

### What makes data suitable for this setting

Given this generative framing, the data requirements are different from the classification setting:

1. **Coverage of the fixed-point manifold**: The observations must collectively cover all modes of the fixed-point distribution. Sparse coverage of some modes means those attractors are poorly constrained.

2. **Observations must be *informative about `z`* but not fully determining**: If `x_obs` fully determines `z*` (noiseless, complete), there is no posterior uncertainty to model. The interesting regime is where `x_obs` is ambiguous — consistent with multiple `z*` values — and the prior does real work.

3. **The noise/corruption process should be known or learnable**: The likelihood `p(x_obs | z)` must be specified or jointly learned. If the corruption process is arbitrary and unmodeled, the likelihood is uninformative and inference reduces to sampling the prior.

4. **Fixed points must be stable under the observation model**: If the partial observation `x_obs` alone would push the solver toward an unrealistic `z*` (outside the fixed-point manifold), the refinement step is essential to "re-anchor" the latent.

---

### The fundamental insight

What you are describing is essentially a **score-based or energy-based generative model where the energy landscape is defined by the DEQ's fixed-point structure**. The multi-modal fixed-point distribution plays the role of the prior, and inference is posterior sampling in that prior conditioned on noisy observations. This is a coherent and principled framework — it is close in spirit to diffusion models (where the prior is a learned denoising process) and to implicit neural representations (where the latent is defined by a self-consistency condition rather than an explicit embedding). The DEQ's inductive bias — that realistic states satisfy a self-consistency equation — is a strong and potentially very useful prior for physical or structured data where such equilibrium conditions are meaningful.
